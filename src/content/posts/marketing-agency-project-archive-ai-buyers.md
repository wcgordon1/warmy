Every agency I know has the same quiet asset.

Years of briefs. Thousands of Slack threads with clients. Revision rounds. Project tickets. Email chains about why the launch slipped. Nobody thinks of it as worth anything. It is just the mess you keep in case a client asks what happened in 2022.

AI companies now think that mess is worth paying for.

## Agencies are on the list

This is not a guess. [Scale AI's data partnership page](https://scale.com/data-partnership) lists Marketing Agencies among its industries of interest, right next to Managed IT Services, Law Firms and Field Services. It says it wants records from the tools teams already use: Slack and Teams, Gmail and Outlook, Docs and Drive, Jira and Asana, HubSpot and Salesforce.

Its FAQ explains why. AI companies want their models to be more useful to certain roles, and it names marketers as an example. A model learns how marketing work gets done by reading how marketing work actually got done.

[Polyshares](https://polyshares.com/track-record), another buyer, publishes a short list of licenses it says it has closed. One is a global marketing agency at $110,000, for a perpetual exclusive license to its operational record. That figure is self-reported by the buyer. It is one deal, not a price list.

So the demand is real. The harder question is what you are actually allowed to sell.

## What an agency archive holds

Open your tools and you will find roughly this:

- **Briefs and strategy docs.** What the client asked for, and how you turned it into a plan.
- **Client Slack channels.** Often Slack Connect, so the client's own people are typing in them.
- **Revision rounds.** Feedback, pushback, the fourth version of the homepage hero.
- **Project tickets.** Jira, Asana, ClickUp or Linear. Scopes, estimates, what slipped and why.
- **Email.** Kickoffs, approvals, escalations, invoices, the "quick question" that became a change order.
- **Your own CRM.** Proposals, pitches you won and lost, notes on prospects.
- **Internal channels.** Staffing, process arguments, retros, how you train a new account manager.
- **Client data.** Ad account exports, analytics, email lists, customer files you were given to do the work.

To a buyer, the best of this is the reasoning. Why a draft was rejected. How a scope was negotiated. What the senior strategist said that changed the plan. That is the stuff a model cannot invent.

To you, most of it has a problem.

## The big issue: much of it is not yours

An agency does work for other companies. That means a large share of your archive is your client's information, sitting in your systems, under your client's contract.

Pull your last few master services agreements and look for three clauses.

**Confidentiality.** Most MSAs say you will use the client's confidential information only to do the work. A launch plan, a pricing test, a positioning memo, a messy internal debate the client had in your shared channel. Licensing that to an AI company is a new use. Your contract probably did not allow for it.

**Ownership of work product.** Many agency contracts assign the deliverables to the client once paid. The final campaign, the copy, the designs may legally belong to them, not you.

**Data processing.** If you handled your client's customer data, you may have signed a data processing agreement. Under the GDPR, a processor works [only on documented instructions from the controller](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-28-gdpr/). US state privacy laws have similar rules for service providers. Selling that data to a third party is about as far from "the client's instructions" as you can get.

Buyers know this. Scale says employee and customer identities never leave the partner's environment, and that it de-identifies everything before extraction. Polyshares says a specialist partner strips names, emails, phone numbers, account numbers and addresses before it sees anything.

That helps with privacy. It does not fix the contract. A client's product launch with the names removed is still the client's confidential plan. De-identification answers "can anyone tell who this is?" It does not answer "did they agree to this?"

Records with other people's information in them get scrutiny even in the most motivated sale. When Spirit Airlines' internal records went to auction in bankruptcy, Google won with a $10 million bid, and a flight attendants' union objected on privacy grounds. Court approval was still pending at last report. Your clients will care at least as much as a union does.

I am not a lawyer and this is not legal advice. Have yours read the actual contracts. But go in expecting that client material is the hard part.

## What you can likely license, and what you can't

Here is how I would sort it before calling anyone.

**Probably yours to license (after de-identification):**

- Internal Slack or Teams channels with no client present. How your team plans, staffs, argues and decides.
- Your own project management history, especially scopes, estimates against actuals, and how work moved through stages.
- Your own new-business record. Proposals, pitch decks you wrote, win and loss notes. Prospect names come out.
- Internal SOPs, templates, training docs and retros you wrote yourself.

**Possible, with written client consent:**

- Shared client channels and email threads.
- Briefs and revision history for specific accounts.
- Older work for clients who are gone, if the contract allows it and you can still reach them. Contracts often outlive the relationship.

Some clients will say yes, especially if the material is old, anonymized and the scope is narrow. Ask in writing. Do not bury it in a renewal.

**Leave it out:**

- Your clients' customer data. Lists, ad audiences, analytics exports, CRM imports.
- Anything covered by a data processing agreement.
- Anything under an NDA that does not clearly allow it.
- Regulated material: health, financial, children's data, wherever a client handed it over.

Do this honestly and the licensable pile gets smaller. It also gets cleaner. A small, clean record a buyer can actually use beats a big one nobody can sign off on.

## Size matters to most buyers

Agencies run small. That is the second filter.

Buyers publish their own lines. Scale says 40+ employees is its preferred starting point, though it reviews case by case. micro1 looks for 30+. Mode wants 30+ for most companies. Endless targets roughly 30+. Replay's tiers start at 20 to 50 employees. Handshake AI asks for 20+ employees and 3+ years in business.

Polyshares takes a different view. It says the length and texture of the record matter more than headcount or revenue.

Money ranges vary just as much. Replay publishes $10K to $100K for companies of 20 to 50 people. Scale lists $10K to $1M+ as illustrative. micro1 and Polyshares both talk in six and seven figures for qualified companies. These are the buyers' own published ranges. None of them is a promise about your agency.

If you are a 12-person studio, most of these doors are closed today. If you are 60 people with eight years in Slack and Asana, you are squarely in range for several.

## A sane first step

Do not start by exporting anything. Start with a page of notes.

1. **List your systems.** Slack, email, project tool, CRM, Drive. For each, write how many years of history you have.
2. **Split shared from internal.** In Slack, count client-facing channels and internal ones. Most agencies are surprised how much is shared.
3. **Read five contracts.** Pick your biggest current clients and two old ones. Note the confidentiality, ownership and data processing clauses.
4. **Sort into the three piles above.** Yours, needs consent, leave out.
5. **Then look at buyers.** If you want to see who is active and what each one pays for, here is a [plain rundown of who actually buys company data, from AI labs to investors](https://howmuchismycompanydataworth.org/blog/posts/who-buys-company-data). If you want a quicker read on fit, there is [a nine-question estimator that matches a company to likely buyers](https://howmuchismycompanydataworth.org/estimate). It asks about size, years of records, where they live and what limits sharing.

That last question is the one that matters for agencies. Answer it honestly.

---

## The short version

AI buyers want to know how marketing work really happens. Your archive shows that better than almost anything.

But an agency archive is mostly other people's business. Your internal record is yours. Client material needs client consent. Client customer data stays out.

Sort it first. Read the contracts. Then decide whether the part that is truly yours is worth a conversation.
